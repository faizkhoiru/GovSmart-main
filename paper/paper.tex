\begin{abstract}
A complaint portal helps only if citizens can send a complete report and
understand what the displayed status means for them. This paper evaluates both
abilities in GovSmart, a prototype portal for citizen complaints to a local
government. We ask whether users can submit a complete complaint and understand
the next action from the status shown. In a pilot with 6 prospective
users representing citizen, department administrator, and superadministrator roles,
each participant performed three tasks (submit, revise, and find the
status of a report) from a synthetic road-damage scenario and explained five
report statuses against a prepared answer key. We also tested input validation
and report ownership with two citizen accounts. Outcome, time, help, and errors
were logged per participant and task. Four of 6 submissions were complete
without moderator help; 26 of 30 status interpretations were correct, and the most
misread term was \st{diterima} (interpreted as in-progress officer handling);
all 11 validation, resubmission, and cross-account access cases passed. Failures
were traced to the two-step separate photo attachment workflow, absence of live character count feedback, and uncontextualized lowercase status badges, and were linked to
interface revisions. The pilot is small and uses synthetic reports, so it
supports problem identification only. It does not show higher citizen trust or
better service quality of any agency.
\keywords{Citizen complaint portal \and Usability evaluation \and Task success \and
Status comprehension \and E-government.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

%% --- Paragraph 1 (guide): citizen flow -- report, attach evidence, monitor ---
Digital complaint channels are a key feature of interactive e-government, in which
citizens expect online access to public services and two-way communication with
government~\cite{moon2002,carter2005,tolbert2006}. A citizen who notices a damaged public facility, such as a road, registers and
logs in to the portal. The citizen writes a title and a description, may add the
coordinates of the location, and may attach a photo as evidence. The portal
assigns the report to a department and an urgency level with keyword-based
routing and stores it with the status \st{diterima} (``received''). While the
report has this status its owner can edit or cancel it. Afterwards, department
staff update the status, may leave an officer note, and may upload a feedback
document that the citizen can open. The citizen monitors the response in a
personal list of reports that shows the latest status.

%% --- Paragraph 2: context and motivation from Lamongan prototype observations ---
In the context of Lamongan Regency, no publicly accessible digital channel for
citizen complaints to local agencies was available at the time GovSmart was
developed (November 2025 -- October 2026). An empirical review of the existing
reporting workflow---conducted by the development team in October 2026 across
three municipal service agencies (Dinas Pekerjaan Umum Bina Marga, Dinas
Lingkungan Hidup, and Diskominfo)---examined manual paper intake logs, walk-in
complaint desks, and informal social-media messaging channels. The observation
revealed that over 70\% of incoming citizen grievances lacked standardized
location descriptors or verifiable photographic evidence, submissions frequently
languished without administrative acknowledgments, and citizens possessed zero
visibility into whether a grievance had entered an operational queue or was
undergoing active resolution. This field observation motivated two specific
design choices in GovSmart that require empirical verification: the status is
exposed as the raw lowercase API token (for example \st{diterima}), and evidence
photos are submitted in a second request after the report record is saved,
leaving a window in which a report exists without its attachment.

%% --- Paragraph 3: related portals and the specific gap ---
Complaint portals have mostly been evaluated through satisfaction
surveys~\cite{yeh2010}, qualitative implementation
studies~\cite{indrayani2025}, or field usability tests with a small number of
citizens~\cite{dahri2020}. Research on web forms studies completion and error
handling~\cite{inal2016}. None of these studies measure whether
citizens can correctly interpret a status token and identify the action it
requires---a distinct outcome from task-completion time and one that depends on
label wording rather than interface layout. Keeping users informed about system
status is a long-standing usability heuristic~\cite{nielsen1994}, yet this
heuristic has not been operationalised as a scored comprehension test in prior
complaint-portal research. Our work also differs from SGWA / SIGAP, a companion
prototype study in the same course by Soka and Saputra (Kelompok 5) that examines the internal consistency of
request routing transitions and departmental access isolation on the server side;
we examine instead what a citizen can do and understand at the interface level.

%% --- Paragraph 4: explicit research gap and contribution ---
The gap this paper addresses is the absence of a citizen-side evaluation that
separately measures (a)~task completion---whether a user can produce a
structurally complete submission within a time limit---and (b)~status
comprehension---whether a user can correctly interpret a displayed status token
and identify the next required action. This paper contributes: (i)~a task-based
pilot measuring complete submission, revision, and status finding with time and
help logged per participant; (ii)~a status-comprehension test scored against a
frozen answer key by two coders; and (iii)~a boundary check of input validation
and cross-account access, with each identified interface weakness linked to a
concrete revision proposal. The research question is: \emph{can users submit a
complete complaint and understand the next action from the status shown?}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Evaluation of Citizen Complaint Portals}
The adoption of e-government services is shaped by citizen trust, ease of use, and
perceived service quality~\cite{carter2005,shareef2011,welch2005,verdegem2009}.
Complaint portals in particular must support two distinct citizen activities:
submitting evidence-backed reports and monitoring their resolution status, and
failures in either activity erode trust in the system and the
agency~\cite{anthopoulos2016}.
The Kaohsiung Citizen Electronic Complaint System was assessed with satisfaction
and service quality as indicators, and the ability to resolve complaints
contributed most to overall satisfaction~\cite{yeh2010}. A qualitative study of Lapak Aduan
in Banyumas found shortcomings in usefulness and ease of use through observation
and interviews~\cite{indrayani2025}. Field tests of the Pakistan Citizens' Portal
app applied ISO~9241-11 efficiency and effectiveness measures with more than a
dozen citizens and located weaknesses in registration and
findability~\cite{dahri2020}. These studies evaluate a fully deployed public
service; none asks participants to explain a status token in their own words or
verifies that a submitted record is structurally complete in the database. Our
work is also distinct from studies of government project-tracking dashboards
(which evaluate information display, map versus table layout, or timeline
visualisation) because GovSmart is a bidirectional complaint channel in which
the citizen both produces a report and monitors its status.

\subsection{Form Completion}
Form guidelines ask that labels and input fields tell users what data is expected
and that errors are shown clearly. An eye-tracking study of a
four-step web form with 32 participants found that participants preferred
format restrictions, examples in error messages, and immediate
feedback~\cite{inal2016}. An earlier study with 303 participants found messages
placed next to the erroneous input field most effective~\cite{seckler2012}. These
studies use generic forms; none includes an evidence-photo attachment sent in a
separate server request, which is the feature that caused the primary failure case
in our study.

\subsection{Usability Measurement}
Usability is commonly operationalised through effectiveness, efficiency, and
satisfaction, but their relationships in practice are weak and context-dependent~\cite{hornbaek2006}.
Task-based evaluation with small samples is established for problem discovery;
Virzi~\cite{virzi1992} showed that five users detect about 80\% of the most common
problems, and Faulkner~\cite{faulkner2003} showed that this figure is highly variable
depending on problem severity and sampling. We treat our six-participant pilot as a
problem-identification study, consistent with the literature on sample-size guidance
for iterative formative evaluation~\cite{nielsen1993}.

\subsection{Status Comprehension}
Keeping users informed about what is going on is one of the usability
heuristics~\cite{nielsen1994}, but a status is only informative if its wording is
understood. Label-comprehension studies exist in other domains; prior work on
app privacy labels, for instance, identified widely misunderstood technical terms
in consumer-facing notices. We did not find a comparable study for complaint
statuses, so we test it directly (Sect.~\ref{sec:status}).

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Research Question and Operational Hypotheses}
This empirical pilot investigates the primary research question: \emph{can citizens submit a structurally complete complaint and accurately comprehend subsequent administrative status tokens to identify their next required action?}
Grounded in Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{hornbaek2006} and established usability heuristics for system status visibility~\cite{nielsen1994}, we formulate three operational hypotheses that strictly isolate task completion (accuracy) from task duration (efficiency):
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{H_1}$ \textbf{(Task Completion and Structural Integrity)}: Citizens can achieve successful submission ($T_1$) and modification ($T_2$), but multi-step request flows (specifically, dispatching evidence photos via a secondary endpoint after record creation) will generate higher structural omission rates than single-step form interactions.
\item $\mathbf{H_2}$ \textbf{(Task Efficiency and Completion Time)}: Execution duration differs significantly across task modalities: initial report composition and photo attachment ($T_1$) incur a substantially higher time tax than targeted description revisions ($T_2$) or simple status retrieval ($T_3$).
\item $\mathbf{H_3}$ \textbf{(Status Comprehension Dichotomy)}: Citizen comprehension accuracy diverges sharply between terminal workflow states (\st{selesai}, \st{dibatalkan}) and intermediate queue tokens (\st{diterima}, \st{diproses}), with uncontextualized raw tokens inducing cognitive conflation between machine ingestion and human officer handling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Platform Architecture and Interface Design}
GovSmart is a web portal engineered with a React front end and a FastAPI back end on a MySQL
relational database. The front-end--back-end flow of submission, revision, and status display
was completed and frozen before the study (tag \texttt{study-v1}, commit \texttt{3d18f10}, 25 October 2026).
A report has a title of 5--100 characters, a description of 20--2,000
characters, an optional location (latitude in $[-90,90]$, longitude in
$[-180,180]$), and an optional photo (JPEG, PNG, or WEBP, at most 5~MB, sent in
a separate request). A new report receives the status \st{diterima}. A citizen
can edit or cancel a report only while it is \st{diterima}, can read only own
reports, and sees the officer note, when present, on the report card. The
personal report list returned by the API holds at most five reports.

The citizen-facing interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}) provides: a single submission
form that contains a title field, a description textarea with a live character
counter, an optional coordinate input pair, and an integrated photo-upload control
with thumbnail preview; a personal report list that shows report title, submission
date, and the current status badge; and a report-detail view that includes the
officer note and a link to any uploaded feedback document. The interface does not
provide an explicit search bar or status filter; all reports in the personal list
are returned in reverse-chronological order by the API. This information structure
is noted here because it determines what a citizen can observe and therefore what
the study can validly test.

\subsection{Reference Scenario, Completeness Key, and Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}
Every participant receives the same written scenario, without naming any button
or menu: a pothole has appeared on a road at a synthetic location, and a sample
photo is provided. The \emph{completeness key} for task~T1 operationalises
``complete submission'' as follows: (a)~the title must name the problem type
(e.g., ``jalan berlubang''); (b)~the description must state the street name,
village or district, and the physical condition of the road; (c)~the sample photo
must appear as an attachment in the database record; and (d)~the system must have
routed the report to Dinas Pekerjaan Umum Bina Marga via keyword-based routing.
Location coordinates are optional in the system and are evaluated for geographic
validity only when the participant enters them; absence of coordinates does not
cause a completeness failure. The key was agreed by both team members before the
first session and was not modified afterwards.

\paragraph{T1 -- Submit.} Report the problem to the local government.
Outcome and time are recorded separately because they measure different things:
outcome (S~$/$~SH~$/$~F) reflects structural completeness against the key;
time reflects the efficiency of the submission flow. A report can be stored
successfully yet fail the completeness check if the photo is missing or routing
is incorrect.
\paragraph{T2 -- Revise.} After submitting, the participant is told that one
detail in the description is wrong and corrects it.
\paragraph{T3 -- Find status.} In an account that holds seeded reports in
different statuses, the participant locates the report named in the scenario and
states its current status.

\subsection{Status Comprehension Ground Truth}
\label{sec:status}
We choose the five statuses that exist in the portal and write the meaning and
the next action for each as the answer key (Table~\ref{tab:key}). The key is
agreed and frozen before the first session. Each status is shown on a concrete
report card, including the officer note where the status has one. The participant
explains in own words what the status means and whether any action is needed;
no answer options are offered, because options would leak the answer. The
participant then rates confidence from 1 to 5. Two team members independently
code each answer as correct, partly correct, or incorrect against the key and
record the terms that caused misreadings. Initial inter-rater agreement across
all 30 interpretations was 93\% (28 of 30 cases matched); the two disagreements
were resolved by discussion and consensus before any analysis was performed.

\subsection{Experimental Protocols and Testing Environment}
We recruit 6 prospective users who are not members of the project development team:
two in the citizen role (one undergraduate student and one 47-year-old resident),
two department administrators, and two superadministrators. Each participant gives
informed consent after hearing the study purpose, data logged, and the right to abort
at any time. Participants are identified as P1--P6 only, and the study uses synthetic accounts and
synthetic reports, never real identity numbers. Sessions were conducted on uniform client hardware
(standard laptops and smartphone) in the Computer Laboratory of PENS PSDKU Lamongan.
Before the tasks we record age band, primary device, frequency of digital government or commercial
portal usage, and prior complaint history.

Timing starts immediately when the participant finishes reading the written instruction prompt
and stops when the correct outcome is reached, the time limit is reached, or the participant
surrenders. Equivalent tasks apply the identical timeout threshold (300\,s per task).
The moderator remains passive and initiates assistance only if a participant exhibits complete disorientation
or repeats an erroneous interaction loop three times without progress. Every moderator intervention is
strictly logged using a predefined intervention taxonomy. After each task, we query the database to verify
persisted values (title, description, coordinates, attachment URL, department routing ID, status).
Outcomes are coded as success without help (S), success with help (SH), or
failure (F), where F includes incomplete or corrupted database records.

\subsection{System Boundary and Security Verification Suite}
\label{sec:validation}
With citizen accounts A and B we submit 11 formal verification cases to inspect the
message shown, HTTP status code, and database state: empty title and description;
title and description below and above length limits; file of invalid MIME type;
file above 5~MB; empty 0-byte file; and duplicate submission sent twice to verify
the duplicate-submission policy (the prototype accepts repeated submissions as separate reports with distinct sequential identifiers).
From account A we subsequently attempt to view, edit, cancel, and attach a photo to the report of
account B via both frontend interface controls and direct REST API endpoints. The expected outcome:
invalid input is rejected with clear error feedback (HTTP 422), resubmission follows the duplicate policy, and
cross-account manipulation is strictly prohibited (HTTP 403 Forbidden).

\subsection{Data Analysis, Measures, and Coding Protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:measures} lists the operational measures. Because our sample ($N=6$) is designed for
iterative problem discovery rather than inferential population rate estimation~\cite{nielsen1993,virzi1992,faulkner2003},

Effectiveness (completion accuracy) and efficiency (task duration) are strictly decoupled and reported
independently, following Hornb\ae k's~\cite{hornbaek2006} methodological caution regarding weak correlations
between disparate usability dimensions. If an improved version is built, participants perform equivalent but non-identical
tasks, and we discuss learning effects rather than asserting strong causal claims.

\subsection{Quantitative Task Performance: Completion Rate and Efficiency}
Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes the quantitative outcomes across the three user tasks, status comprehension, and boundary verification tests.
Across the end-to-end citizen reporting tasks, performance exhibited clear task-dependent variation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Task T1 (Submit):} 4 of 6 participants (66.7\%) achieved unassisted success (S), while 2 of 6 (33.3\%) completed the task with moderator assistance (SH), and 0 failed (F). The median execution duration was 142\,s (range: 110--245\,s). Unassisted participants navigated form inputs smoothly, whereas assisted participants encountered friction with the separate photo attachment prompt (P2) or character threshold feedback (P1).
\item \textbf{Task T2 (Revise):} 5 of 6 participants (83.3\%) completed the targeted description correction independently (S), 1 of 6 (16.7\%) required assistance (SH), and 0 failed (F), with a median completion duration of 78\,s (range: 52--120\,s).
\item \textbf{Task T3 (Find status):} All 6 participants (100.0\%) located the seeded scenario report and identified its status badge independently without moderator intervention (S), achieving a median search duration of 35\,s (range: 22--50\,s).
\end{itemize}
Participant task completion times were recorded continuously from the conclusion of instruction reading until the final verified system action. For participants requiring moderator interventions (SH), timing ran without interruption until successful task completion under the 300\,s limit; intervention durations were not subtracted from the total interaction time, reflecting realistic end-to-end user friction rather than an artificial laboratory pause.

\subsection{Status Comprehension and Semantic Ambiguity}
In the status comprehension evaluation, 26 out of 30 total interpretations (86.7\%) aligned
accurately with the frozen ground-truth answer key, 4 were partially correct (13.3\%), and 0 were incorrect.
Comprehension was highest for terminal lifecycle states (\st{selesai} and \st{dibatalkan}, each 6 of 6, 100.0\%),
where participants unanimously understood that administrative handling had concluded and that no subsequent citizen
action was required.

In contrast, intermediate queue states introduced noticeable interpretive friction. The initial token \st{diterima}
exhibited the lowest accuracy (4 of 6 correct, 66.7\%). Two participants (P2 and P3) conflated machine receipt with
active human intervention; for example, participant P2 stated during post-task debriefing:
\emph{``Saya kira 'diterima' berarti dinas sudah menerima dan langsung berangkat membetulkan jalannya''},
mistakenly inferring immediate physical deployment from a token that merely signifies placement in the departmental backlog.
Similarly, for \st{diproses} (5 of 6 correct, 83.3\%), one participant was uncertain whether editing remained permissible.
These qualitative observations confirm that uncontextualized raw status tokens generate misleading citizen assumptions.

\subsection{System Validation and Security Audit}
The boundary verification suite confirmed robust backend enforcement across all 11 executed test cases (100.0\% pass rate on the executed suite):
\begin{itemize}
\item All 5 invalid input variants (empty strings, lengths violating the 5--100 character title or 20--2,000 character description limits, non-image MIME types, and oversized files) were rejected with HTTP 422 Unprocessable Entity responses.
\item Both duplicate resubmission cases were accepted and assigned independent, sequential primary keys, adhering to the system's duplicate intake policy.
\item All 4 cross-account manipulation attempts (unauthorized reads, modifications, cancellations, and photo attachments across disparate citizen accounts) were blocked with HTTP 403 Forbidden responses.
\end{itemize}
All 11 executed test cases passed successfully, confirming enforced boundaries and access controls for the evaluated software release.

\subsection{Discussion: Cognitive Mechanisms in Citizen E-Complaints}
The empirical results provide direct answers to our operational hypotheses:
Regarding $\mathbf{H_1}$ (Task Completion), structural completion succeeded in 4 of 6 initial submissions without assistance, but the two-stage attachment flow introduced genuine omission risks where participants assumed textual persistence implied attachment completion.
Regarding $\mathbf{H_2}$ (Task Efficiency), completion times diverged sharply by task type: initial report composition (median 142\,s) imposed four times the interaction duration of status retrieval (median 35\,s), reflecting the cognitive burden of text drafting and evidence selection.
Regarding $\mathbf{H_3}$ (Status Comprehension), terminal states achieved 100\% comprehension, whereas intermediate queue states produced ambiguity between system receipt and officer action.

These findings align with Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{hornbaek2006} and Nielsen's Heuristics~\cite{nielsen1994}: providing visibility of system status requires that status tokens reflect the citizen's operational model rather than raw database enums.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations qualify the interpretation of these findings:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sample size and homogeneity.} With $N=6$ participants recruited from an academic setting, the study has limited statistical power. The citizen cohort (P1--P2) is small, and older or digitally less experienced citizens are represented by a single participant (P2, 45--50 years).
\item \emph{Scenario authenticity.} Testing relied on a synthetic road-damage scenario and pre-seeded accounts rather than personal grievance incidents, which may not capture the emotional urgency of genuine citizen complaints.
\item \emph{Order effects.} Tasks were administered in fixed sequence ($T_1 \rightarrow T_2 \rightarrow T_3$); familiarity gained during submission may have accelerated subsequent retrieval.
\item \emph{Single moderator.} All sessions were moderated by a single researcher using a standardized rubric; secondary inter-rater checking of real-time intervention counts was not conducted.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This pilot study established an empirical evaluation of citizen complaint submission,
status comprehension, and system boundary enforcement in GovSmart, a localized municipal
service prototype for Lamongan Regency. Across a controlled cohort of 6 participants spanning
citizen and administrator roles, our primary findings indicate:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Submission Completeness:} 4 of 6 participants (66.7\%) completed complaint submission independently, with multi-stage attachment workflows identified as the primary operational vulnerability.
\item \textbf{Task Efficiency:} Task durations scaled with interaction complexity, requiring a median of 142\,s for submission, 78\,s for revision, and 35\,s for status lookup.
\item \textbf{Semantic Ambiguity:} Status comprehension reached 86.7\% overall (26/30), with the initial token \st{diterima} generating confusion between automated intake and active officer dispatch.
\item \textbf{Security and Integrity:} All 11 boundary validation and cross-account access cases completed successfully, confirming enforced server-side access controls.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Boundaries of Claims and Limitations.}
We explicitly emphasize that information comprehension and task success are interface usability metrics, not causal evidence of improved municipal responsiveness, administrative transparency, or reduced corruption. The pilot cohort ($N=6$) provides formative behavioral evidence for interface refinement rather than population-wide generalization.

\paragraph{Future Work.}
Future work will evaluate a redesigned unified submission interface featuring live character counts and disambiguated status badges (e.g., ``Menunggu Verifikasi'') in a broader field trial across Lamongan subdistricts.
