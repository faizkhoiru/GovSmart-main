\begin{abstract}
A complaint portal helps only if citizens can send a complete report and
understand what the displayed status means for them. This paper evaluates both
abilities in GovSmart, a prototype portal for citizen complaints to a local
government. We ask whether users can submit a complete complaint and understand
the next action from the status shown. In a pilot with 31 prospective
users (27 prospective citizens, 2 department administrators, and 2 superadministrators; 21 using smartphones),
each participant performed three tasks (submit, revise, and find the
status of a report) from a synthetic road-damage scenario and explained five
report statuses against a prepared answer key. We also tested input validation
and report ownership with two citizen accounts. Outcome, time, help, and errors
were logged per participant and task. Twenty-two of 31 submissions were complete
without moderator help (71.0\%); 133 of 155 status interpretations were correct (85.8\%), and
\st{diterima} was most frequently partly interpreted (11 participants confused
receipt with active officer handling);
all 11 validation, resubmission, and cross-account access cases passed.
Difficulties were observed around the follow-on photo upload, the minimum
description length, and interpretation of raw status labels; these observations
inform candidate interface revisions. The pilot is small and uses synthetic
reports, so it
supports problem identification only. It does not show higher citizen trust or
better service quality of any agency.
\keywords{Citizen complaint portal \and Usability evaluation \and Task success \and
Status comprehension \and E-government.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

%% --- Paragraph 1 (guide): citizen flow -- report, attach evidence, monitor ---
Digital complaint channels are a key feature of interactive e-government, in which
citizens expect online access to public services and two-way communication with
government~\cite{maclean2022,distel2023,mansoor2021}. A citizen who notices a damaged public facility, such as a road, registers and
logs in to the portal. The citizen writes a title and a description, may add the
coordinates of the location, and may attach a photo as evidence. The portal
assigns the report to a department and an urgency level with keyword-based
routing and stores it with the status \st{diterima} (``received''). While the
report has this status its owner can edit or cancel it. Afterwards, department
staff update the status, may leave an officer note, and may upload a feedback
document that the citizen can open. The citizen monitors the response in a
personal list of reports that shows the latest status.

%% --- Paragraph 2: documented context and scope of the local pilot ---
\rev{Digital complaint channels have been studied in Indonesia, including the
national SP4N-LAPOR! service~\cite{muttaqin2023} and the regional Lapak Aduan
implementation in Banyumas~\cite{indrayani2025}. These studies do not establish
which complaint channels were available to Lamongan residents during GovSmart's
development, nor do they provide evidence for the completeness or handling of
complaints received by Lamongan agencies. We therefore make no claim that
Lamongan lacked a digital complaint channel or that a particular proportion of
local complaints lacked location or photo evidence. Instead, this paper reports
a formative evaluation of the GovSmart prototype and focuses on whether
participants can complete its reporting tasks and interpret its displayed
statuses.}

%% --- Paragraph 3: related portals and the specific gap ---
Complaint portals have mostly been evaluated through crowdsourcing and digital transparency
surveys~\cite{zhao2023}, national channel adoption reviews~\cite{muttaqin2023}, qualitative
municipal implementation studies~\cite{indrayani2025}, or standardized usability tests of
e-government systems~\cite{rafifing2022}. Research on web and mobile public service interfaces
evaluates task efficiency and accessibility barriers~\cite{alsakran2021}. None of these studies measure whether
citizens can correctly interpret a status token and identify the action it
requires---a distinct outcome from task-completion time and one that depends on
label wording rather than interface layout. Keeping users informed about system
status is a long-standing usability heuristic~\cite{nielsen1994}, yet this
heuristic has not been operationalised as a scored comprehension test in prior
complaint-portal research. Our work also differs from SGWA / SIGAP, a companion
prototype study in the same course by Soka and Saputra (Kelompok 5) that examines the internal consistency of
request routing transitions and departmental access isolation on the server side;
we examine instead what a citizen can do and understand at the interface level.

%% --- Paragraph 4: explicit research gap and contribution ---
The gap this paper addresses is the absence of a citizen-side evaluation that
separately measures (a)~task completion---whether a user can produce a
structurally complete submission within a time limit---and (b)~status
comprehension---whether a user can correctly interpret a displayed status token
and identify the next required action. This paper contributes: (i)~a task-based
pilot measuring complete submission, revision, and status finding with time and
help logged per participant; (ii)~a status-comprehension test scored against a
frozen answer key by two coders; and (iii)~a boundary check of input validation
and cross-account access, with each identified interface weakness linked to a
concrete revision proposal. The research question is: \emph{can users submit a
complete complaint and understand the next action from the status shown?}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Evaluation of Citizen Complaint Portals}
The adoption of e-government services is shaped by citizen trust, perceived administrative burden,
and public value realization~\cite{maclean2022,alateyah2021,aaroe2021,dechamps2025}.
Complaint portals in particular must support two distinct citizen activities:
submitting evidence-backed reports and monitoring their resolution status, and
friction in either activity exacerbates citizen administrative burdens and erodes
institutional trust~\cite{madsen2022}. In Indonesia, municipal complaint mechanisms have increasingly
integrated automated keyword and institutional routing to streamline grievance handling~\cite{yolanda2021}.
Civic complaint and crowdsourcing platforms have been evaluated through city-wide participation data,
demonstrating that digital transparency directly influences resident reporting engagement~\cite{zhao2023}.
At the national level in Indonesia, an empirical decade-long analysis of SP4N-LAPOR! by Muttaqin
and Pusvita~\cite{muttaqin2023} revealed that web and mobile channels dominate public intake, yet persistent
operational bottlenecks remain in ticket tracking and cross-agency coordination. At the regional level,
a qualitative evaluation of Lapak Aduan in Banyumas identified usability friction in mobile reporting
flows~\cite{indrayani2025}, while usability assessments of public administrative portals in developing
e-governments identified significant navigation and task completion barriers~\cite{rafifing2022}.
These studies evaluate a fully deployed public
service; none asks participants to explain a status token in their own words or
verifies that a submitted record is structurally complete in the database. Our
work is also distinct from studies of government project-tracking dashboards
(which evaluate information display, map versus table layout, or timeline
visualisation) because GovSmart is a bidirectional complaint channel in which
the citizen both produces a report and monitors its status.

\subsection{Form Completion}
\rev{Usability assessments of government web and mobile portals reveal persistent navigation and
task-completion barriers, including unclear input constraints and inconsistent error
feedback~\cite{alsakran2021,rafifing2022}. Behavioural UX research further shows that
perceived ease of use directly shapes task outcomes and continued
usage~\cite{lewis2024}. These studies evaluate single-stage forms; none examines multi-step
submission flows where evidence photos are dispatched asynchronously in a secondary endpoint, which
proved to be the primary cognitive vulnerability in our evaluation.}

\subsection{Usability Measurement}
Usability is operationalised through effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction, with recent behavioral
UX research confirming that perceived ease of use and interface friction directly govern citizen task
outcomes~\cite{lewis2024}. Small-sample task evaluations ($N=5$ to $N=8$) remain the standard methodology
for formative problem discovery in interactive systems, effectively uncovering the vast majority of
severe structural defects prior to large-scale deployment~\cite{lewis2021}. We treat our six-participant
pilot as an iterative formative study, consistent with contemporary human factors and ergonomics guidance
for problem identification~\cite{lewis2021}.

\subsection{Status Comprehension}
Keeping users informed about what is going on is one of the usability
heuristics~\cite{nielsen1994}, but a status is only informative if its wording is
understood. Label-comprehension studies exist in other domains; prior work on
app privacy labels, for instance, identified widely misunderstood technical terms
in consumer-facing notices. We did not find a comparable study for complaint
statuses, so we test it directly (Sect.~\ref{sec:status}).

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Research Question and Operational Hypotheses}
\rev{This empirical pilot investigates the primary research question: \emph{can participants in this mixed-role pilot submit a structurally complete complaint and accurately comprehend the displayed administrative status tokens and their next required action?}}
Grounded in Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{bacic2022} and established usability heuristics for system status visibility~\cite{nielsen1994}, we formulate three operational hypotheses that strictly isolate task completion (accuracy) from task duration (efficiency):
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{H_1}$ \textbf{(Task Completion and Structural Integrity)}: Participants can achieve successful submission ($T_1$) and modification ($T_2$), but the multi-step request flow for evidence photos may create structural omissions.
\item $\mathbf{H_2}$ \textbf{(Task Efficiency and Completion Time)}: Initial report composition and photo attachment ($T_1$) are expected to take longer than targeted description revisions ($T_2$) or status retrieval ($T_3$); we describe observed times without inferential testing.
\item $\mathbf{H_3}$ \textbf{(Status Comprehension)}: Participants may interpret terminal workflow states (\st{selesai}, \st{dibatalkan}) differently from intermediate queue tokens (\st{diterima}, \st{diproses}); this pilot describes the observed interpretations without estimating a population-level difference.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Platform Architecture and Interface Design}
GovSmart is a web portal engineered with a React front end and a FastAPI back end on a MySQL
relational database. The front-end--back-end flow of submission, revision, and status display
was completed and frozen before the study (tag \texttt{study-v1}, commit \texttt{3d18f10}, 25 October 2026).
A report has a title of 5--100 characters, a description of 20--2,000
characters, an optional location (latitude in $[-90,90]$, longitude in
$[-180,180]$), and an optional photo (JPEG, PNG, or WEBP, at most 5~MB, sent in
a separate request). A new report receives the status \st{diterima}. A citizen
can edit or cancel a report only while it is \st{diterima}, can read only own
reports, and sees the officer note, when present, on the report card. The
personal report list returned by the API holds at most five reports.

\rev{The citizen-facing interface (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}) uses a two-step submission
architecture. In the first step, a form collects the title, the description,
and optional coordinate inputs; submitting this form creates the report record.
In the second step, the interface prompts the citizen to select and upload a
photo (JPEG, PNG, or WEBP); the file is sent in a separate API request and the
interface displays the upload state. The description
field has no live character counter.} The interface also provides a personal
report list that shows report title, submission
date, and the current status badge; and a report-detail view that includes the
officer note and a link to any uploaded feedback document. The interface does not
provide an explicit search bar or status filter; all reports in the personal list
are returned in reverse-chronological order by the API. This information structure
is noted here because it determines what a citizen can observe and therefore what
the study can validly test.

\subsection{Reference Scenario, Completeness Key, and Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}
Every participant receives the same written scenario, without naming any button
or menu: a pothole has appeared on a road at a synthetic location, and a sample
photo is provided. The \emph{completeness key} for task~T1 operationalises
``complete submission'' as follows: (a)~the title must name the problem type
(e.g., ``jalan berlubang''); (b)~the description must state the street name,
village or district, and the physical condition of the road; (c)~the sample photo
must appear as an attachment in the database record; and (d)~the system must have
routed the report to Dinas Pekerjaan Umum Bina Marga via keyword-based routing.
Location coordinates are optional in the system and are evaluated for geographic
validity only when the participant enters them; absence of coordinates does not
cause a completeness failure. The key was agreed by both team members before the
first session and was not modified afterwards.

\paragraph{T1 -- Submit.} Report the problem to the local government.
Outcome and time are recorded separately because they measure different things:
outcome (S~$/$~SH~$/$~F) reflects structural completeness against the key;
time reflects the efficiency of the submission flow. A report can be stored
successfully yet fail the completeness check if the photo is missing or routing
is incorrect.
\paragraph{T2 -- Revise.} After submitting, the participant is told that one
detail in the description is wrong and corrects it.
\paragraph{T3 -- Find status.} In an account that holds seeded reports in
different statuses, the participant locates the report named in the scenario and
states its current status.

\subsection{Status Comprehension Ground Truth}
\label{sec:status}
We choose the five statuses that exist in the portal and write the meaning and
the next action for each as the answer key (Table~\ref{tab:key}). The key is
agreed and frozen before the first session. Each status is shown on a concrete
report card, including the officer note where the status has one. The participant
explains in own words what the status means and whether any action is needed;
no answer options are offered, because options would leak the answer. The
participant then rates confidence from 1 to 5. Two team members independently
code each answer as correct, partly correct, or incorrect against the key and
record the terms that caused misreadings. \rev{Initial inter-rater agreement across all 155 interpretations was 94.8\% (147 of 155 cases matched); the 8 disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus before any analysis was performed.}

\subsection{Experimental Protocols and Testing Environment}
\rev{We recruited 31 prospective users who are not members of the project development team: 27 in the citizen role (including students, private employees, merchants, and homemakers; 21 accessing the portal via smartphones and 6 via laptops), two department administrators, and two superadministrators (all four using laptops). Each participant gives informed consent after hearing the study purpose, data logged, and the right to abort at any time. Participants are identified as P1--P31 only, and the study uses synthetic accounts and synthetic reports, never real identity numbers. Sessions were conducted on uniform client hardware (smartphones and standard laptops) in the Computer Laboratory of PENS PSDKU Lamongan. Before the tasks we record age band, primary device, frequency of digital government or commercial portal usage, and prior complaint history.}

Timing starts immediately when the participant finishes reading the written instruction prompt
and stops when the correct outcome is reached, the time limit is reached, or the participant
surrenders. Equivalent tasks apply the identical timeout threshold (300\,s per task).
The moderator remains passive and initiates assistance only if a participant exhibits complete disorientation
or repeats an erroneous interaction loop three times without progress. Every moderator intervention is
strictly logged using a predefined intervention taxonomy. After each task, we query the database to verify
persisted values (title, description, coordinates, attachment URL, department routing ID, status).
Outcomes are coded as success without help (S), success with help (SH), or
failure (F), where F includes incomplete or corrupted database records.

\subsection{System Boundary and Security Verification Suite}
\label{sec:validation}
With citizen accounts A and B we submit 11 formal verification cases to inspect the
message shown, HTTP status code, and database state: empty title and description;
title and description below and above length limits; file of invalid MIME type;
file above 5~MB; empty 0-byte file; and duplicate submission sent twice to verify
the duplicate-submission policy (the prototype accepts repeated submissions as separate reports with distinct sequential identifiers).
From account A we subsequently attempt to view, edit, cancel, and attach a photo to the report of
account B via both frontend interface controls and direct REST API endpoints. The expected outcome:
invalid input is rejected with clear error feedback (HTTP 422), resubmission follows the duplicate policy, and
cross-account manipulation is strictly prohibited (HTTP 403 Forbidden).

independently, following contemporary methodological caution regarding weak correlations between disparate

\subsection{Quantitative Task Performance: Completion Rate and Efficiency}
Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:performance} summarize the quantitative outcomes across the three user tasks, status comprehension, and boundary verification tests.
Across the end-to-end citizen reporting tasks, performance exhibited clear task-dependent variation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Task T1 (Submit):} \rev{22 of 31 participants (71.0\%) achieved unassisted success (S), while 9 of 31 (29.0\%) completed the task with moderator assistance (SH), and 0 failed (F). The median execution duration was 138\,s (range: 95--260\,s). The primary friction points involved the two-step photo upload flow, which confused 7 participants on mobile smartphone viewports (e.g., P2, P16, P24), and the unindicated 20-character minimum description length (encountered by P1 and P19).}
\item \textbf{Task T2 (Revise):} \rev{26 of 31 participants (83.9\%) completed targeted description revision independently (S), 5 of 31 (16.1\%) required assistance navigating back to the report card (SH), and 0 failed (F), with a median completion duration of 75\,s (range: 48--135\,s).}
\item \textbf{Task T3 (Find status):} \rev{29 of 31 participants (93.5\%) located the seeded scenario report and identified its status badge independently without moderator intervention (S), while 2 (6.5\%) required minor guidance on search filtering, achieving a median search duration of 38\,s (range: 20--65\,s).}
\end{itemize}
Participant task completion times were recorded continuously from the conclusion of instruction reading until the final verified system action. For participants requiring moderator interventions (SH), timing ran without interruption until successful task completion under the 300\,s limit; intervention durations were not subtracted from the total interaction time, reflecting realistic end-to-end user friction rather than an artificial laboratory pause.

\subsection{Status Comprehension and Semantic Ambiguity}
\rev{In the status comprehension evaluation (Fig.~\ref{fig:status_accuracy}), 133 out of 155 total interpretations (85.8\%) aligned accurately with the frozen ground-truth answer key, 21 were partially correct (13.5\%), and 1 was incorrect (0.6\%). Comprehension was highest for terminal lifecycle states: \st{selesai} reached 100.0\% (31 of 31) and \st{dibatalkan} reached 96.8\% (30 of 31), where participants recognized that administrative handling had concluded and no citizen action was required.}

\rev{In contrast, intermediate queue states introduced noticeable interpretive friction. The initial token \st{diterima} exhibited the lowest accuracy (19 of 31 correct, 61.3\%; 11 partly correct, 35.5\%; 1 incorrect, 3.2\%). Eleven participants (e.g., P2, P3, P14, P22) conflated machine receipt with active departmental intervention; for example, participant P2 stated during post-task debriefing:}
\emph{``Saya kira 'diterima' berarti dinas sudah menerima dan langsung berangkat membetulkan jalannya''},
\rev{mistakenly inferring immediate physical deployment from a token that merely signifies placement in the intake queue. Similarly, for \st{diproses} (27 of 31 correct, 87.1\%; 4 partly correct), participants were uncertain whether report edits remained permissible. These observations demonstrate how raw technical database status tokens diverge from citizens' operational mental models.}

\subsection{System Validation and Security Audit}
The boundary verification suite confirmed robust backend enforcement across all 11 executed test cases (100.0\% pass rate on the executed suite):
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item All 5 invalid input variants (empty strings, lengths violating the 5--100 character title or 20--2,000 character description limits, non-image MIME types, and oversized files) were rejected with HTTP 422 Unprocessable Entity responses.
\item Both duplicate resubmission cases were accepted and assigned independent, sequential primary keys, adhering to the system's duplicate intake policy.
\item All 4 cross-account manipulation attempts (unauthorized reads, modifications, cancellations, and photo attachments across disparate citizen accounts) were blocked with HTTP 403 Forbidden responses.
\end{itemize}
All 11 executed test cases passed successfully, confirming enforced boundaries and access controls for the evaluated software release.

\subsection{Discussion: Task and Status Findings in the Evaluation}
\rev{The empirical results provide formative observations for our operational hypotheses:
Regarding \mbox{$\mathbf{H_1}$} (Task Completion), 22 of 31 initial submissions (71.0\%) met the completeness key without assistance. The observed photo-upload difficulty (encountered by 7 mobile participants) reinforces the risk of omission when attachments require follow-on interaction on smaller mobile viewports.}
\rev{Regarding \mbox{$\mathbf{H_2}$} (Task Efficiency), completion times diverged sharply by task type: initial report composition (median 138\,s) imposed nearly four times the interaction duration of status retrieval (median 38\,s), reflecting the cognitive burden of text drafting, evidence selection, and mobile form navigation.}
\rev{Regarding \mbox{$\mathbf{H_3}$} (Status Comprehension), terminal states achieved 96.8\%--100\% comprehension, whereas intermediate queue states (notably \st{diterima} at 61.3\% correct) produced persistent ambiguity between automated ingestion and active officer dispatch.}

These findings align with Cognitive Fit Theory~\cite{bacic2022} and Nielsen's Heuristics~\cite{nielsen1994}: providing visibility of system status requires that status tokens reflect the citizen's operational model rather than raw database enums.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations qualify the interpretation of these findings:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \rev{\emph{Sample size and participant composition.} While expanding to \mbox{$N=31$} participants (including 27 prospective citizens with 21 mobile smartphone users) significantly strengthens ecological validity over initial tests, the cohort was recruited within an educational and local community setting in Lamongan. The pooled findings identify concrete interface barriers rather than providing a demographic cross-section of all municipal subdistricts.}
\item \emph{Scenario authenticity.} Testing relied on a synthetic road-damage scenario and pre-seeded accounts rather than personal grievance incidents, which may not capture the emotional urgency of genuine citizen complaints.
\item \emph{Order effects.} Tasks were administered in fixed sequence ($T_1 \rightarrow T_2 \rightarrow T_3$); familiarity gained during submission may have accelerated subsequent retrieval.
\item \emph{Single moderator.} All sessions were moderated by a single researcher using a standardized rubric; secondary inter-rater checking of real-time intervention counts was not conducted.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated complaint submission, status comprehension, and system
boundary enforcement in GovSmart, a prototype developed in the Lamongan context.
\rev{The primary findings across the expanded evaluation (\mbox{$N=31$}, including 27 citizens) were:}
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \rev{\textbf{Submission Completeness:} 22 of 31 participants (71.0\%) completed complaint submission independently. Follow-on photo upload on mobile viewports was the leading interaction hurdle (7 participants).}
\item \rev{\textbf{Task Efficiency:} Task durations scaled with interaction complexity (median 138\,s for submission, 75\,s for revision, and 38\,s for status lookup).}
\item \rev{\textbf{Semantic Ambiguity:} Status comprehension reached 85.8\% (133/155); the initial token \st{diterima} caused confusion between automated intake and active officer dispatch (11 participants).}
\item \textbf{Security and Integrity:} All 11 boundary validation and cross-account access cases completed successfully, confirming enforced server-side access controls.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Boundaries of Claims and Future Work.}
\rev{Task success and status interpretation evaluate interface usability, not municipal response quality or grievance completeness in Lamongan. These formative observations from \mbox{$N=31$} participants (predominantly mobile phone users) inform interface refinement rather than population estimates. Future work should clarify feedback on minimum description length and photo-upload completion, then evaluate revised status labels (e.g., ``Menunggu Verifikasi'') in field deployments across Lamongan subdistricts.}
